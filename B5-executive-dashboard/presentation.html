Executive Dashboard Automation

"An AI that explains weekly KPI changes": what can it honestly say?

1 · The problem

A confident story is not an explanation

"Revenue dropped 8% because of the new pricing page." Fluent, but is it true, or just something that happened the same week?
  • Users: C-level and VP level, who decide whether to act, investigate or wait
  • An analyst reviews the output before executives see it
~25 s. "'Revenue dropped 8% because of the new pricing page' sounds convincing, but it may be coincidence. I'd only trust it if a human reviewed it, so an analyst checks the output first. The users are C-level, who decide whether to act, investigate or wait."
2 · Framing

Three questions, three methods

Is it real?Seasonal baselineNormal range for the KPI, flag only what falls outside it.
Where from?DecompositionSplit into components and segments, rank the contributors.
Why?Causal analysisExperiments or difference-in-differences. Otherwise only a hypothesis.

The language model only writes up the verified results.

~30 s. "I split it into three questions. Is the change real? A seasonal baseline gives a normal range. Where did it come from? We break the KPI into components and segments. And why? That's causal, so it needs an experiment or difference-in-differences. Otherwise the AI offers only a hypothesis. Mostly this is statistics, and the language model only writes up verified results."
3 · What executives would see

A short summary with its evidence, not a story

Week 46: revenue down 8%, outside the expected range of -3% to +3%.

Where: mostly Germany (-14%), lower conversion (2.4% to 1.9%). Austria and Switzerland normal.

Possible cause (hypothesis, not confirmed): new checkout launched in Germany on 4 Nov. Austria kept the old one and shows no drop.

Confidence: medium. Suggested action: investigate checkout, verify tracking.

Illustrative numbers, not real data.

~30 s. "This is what executives would see. Revenue is down 8%, outside the expected range. The drop is mostly in Germany, from lower conversion. The possible cause is a new checkout, labelled as a hypothesis, because Austria kept the old one and shows no drop. Then confidence and a suggested action. The numbers are illustrative."
4 · Guardrails

How it stays honest

  • The language model sees only verified numbers, and every number in the text must trace back to a computed value
  • An analyst reviews before executives see it
  • A simple rule ("flag any change over X%") is the baseline to beat
  • We test on past weeks and count the false alarms
~20 s. "To keep it honest: the language model only sees verified numbers, and every number in the text must trace back to a computed value. An analyst reviews it. And we compare against a simple baseline, like 'flag any change over X percent', and only keep the fancier parts if they reduce false alarms. We'd test this on past weeks."
5 · Data risks

Where it can go wrong

  • Same KPI, different numbers: Meta Ads vs Triple Whale. A team must choose one source of truth
  • Seasonality: a holiday boost is not a success
  • Tracking breaks look like real drops
  • Short history: one to four years of weekly data, so state the confidence
  • No comparison group when a change hits everyone at once
~25 s. "In my marketing work, the same KPI showed different numbers in Meta Ads, the ad platform, and in Triple Whale, an analytics tool for online shops. My team chose one source of truth. A dashboard must not silently mix sources. Other risks: holiday seasons, tracking breaks, and only one to four years of weekly data, so the system must state how confident it is."
6 · What I learned · Difference-in-differences

Estimating a cause without an experiment

ConversionBeforeAfterChange
Germany (new pictures)1.8%2.5%+0.7
Austria (old pictures)1.8%2.3%+0.5
Effect of the change+0.2

Austria shows what would have happened anyway because of Valentine's Day. It assumes both would have moved the same way, and it needs a comparison group. If a change hits everyone at once, there is none.

Corrected assumption: it is not a seasonal model, it is a way to estimate a cause without an experiment.

~30 s. "My new method is difference-in-differences. New product pictures for a Valentine's bundle in Germany, old ones in Austria. Germany rose 0.7 points, Austria 0.5 because of the season, so the effect is 0.2. I first thought it was a seasonal model. It's a way to estimate a cause without an experiment. It assumes both would have moved the same way, and it can't be used when a change hits everyone at once."
