Executive Dashboard Automation

"An AI that explains weekly KPI changes": what can it honestly say?

Irem Baykal · ML problem scoping, with an LLM as thinking partner

1 · The problem

Imagine the AI dashboard says this

"Revenue dropped 8% because of the new pricing page."
  • It sounds convincing, but the AI may only have noticed two things that happened in the same week
  • Is the drop real, where does it come from, did the page cause it? A fluent sentence answers none of these
  • Users: C-level and VP level, who decide whether to act, investigate or wait. An analyst reviews the output first
2 · Framing

Three questions, three methods

Is it real?Seasonal baselineNormal range for the KPI, flag only what falls outside it.
Where from?DecompositionSplit into components and segments, rank the contributors.
Why?Causal analysisExperiments or difference-in-differences. Otherwise only a hypothesis.

The language model only writes up the verified results.

3 · What executives would see

A short summary with its evidence, not a story

Week 46: revenue down 8%, outside the expected range of -3% to +3%.

Where: mostly Germany (-14%), lower conversion (2.4% to 1.9%). Austria and Switzerland normal.

Possible cause (hypothesis, not confirmed): new checkout launched in Germany on 4 Nov. Austria kept the old one and shows no drop.

Confidence: medium. Suggested action: investigate checkout, verify tracking.

Illustrative numbers, not real data.

4 · Guardrails

How it stays honest

  • The language model sees only verified numbers, and every number in the text must trace back to a computed value
  • An analyst reviews before executives see it
  • A simple rule ("flag any change over X%") is the baseline to beat
  • We test on past weeks and count the false alarms
5 · Data risks

Where it can go wrong

  • Same KPI, different numbers: Meta Ads vs Triple Whale. A team must choose one source of truth
  • Seasonality: a holiday boost is not a success
  • Tracking breaks look like real drops
  • Short history: one to four years of weekly data, so state the confidence
  • No comparison group when a change hits everyone at once
6 · What I learned · Difference-in-differences

Estimating a cause without an experiment

Austria shows what would have happened anyway because of the Christmas season. It assumes both would have moved the same way, and it needs a comparison group. If a change hits everyone at once, there is none. (Illustrative numbers.)

Corrected assumption: it is not a seasonal model.

7 · How I used the LLM

An LLM as thinking partner, with me deciding

  • It helped: split "explain a KPI" into three questions with different methods
  • I added: the human-review rule, the source-of-truth problem and the seasonal comparison, all from my own work
  • It corrected me: I wanted to give an LLM two years of KPIs to "analyze". It is unreliable on raw tables, so code computes the numbers and the LLM only writes them up
AI helps once the problem is scoped, the limits are clear, and the "why" has evidence behind it.
